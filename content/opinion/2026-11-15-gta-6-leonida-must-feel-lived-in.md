Every Grand Theft Auto VI discussion I've seen this year eventually turns into the same argument: how big is the map? Is it twice GTA V? Two and a half times? People share fan reconstructions like they're box scores, as if square miles were the thing that makes an open world good.

I think that's the wrong question, and I think Rockstar knows it.

## Size is the easiest thing to fake

Open worlds got big a long time ago. We've all played games with enormous maps that felt like empty parking lots between quest markers. Size is easy to market and easy to measure, which is exactly why it's a poor measure of quality.

The best open-world moments I've had didn't come from scale. They came from detail: a stranger in Red Dead Redemption 2 who remembers how you treated them, a radio host in GTA V mocking something you just did, a random street argument that had nothing to do with me. Those are the moments that make a world feel like it existed before I showed up and will keep going after I leave.

## What "lived in" means for Leonida

Rockstar has given us six regions: Vice City, the Leonida Keys, Grassrivers, Port Gellhorn, Ambrosia and Mount Kalaga National Park. On paper that's variety. The question is whether each one has its own *life*.

Does Port Gellhorn feel like a tourist town that lost its tourists? Does Ambrosia's sugar industry shape who has money and who doesn't? Do the people in the Keys live differently from the people in Vice City, not just look different? Rockstar's character descriptions hint that they're thinking this way, with drug runners in the Keys, real estate and music hustles in Vice City and an industrial power base in Ambrosia. That's world-building through economics and class, and it's exactly the right instinct.

## The trailers already made the argument

Look at what Rockstar chose to show us. Not map overviews or a size comparison, but crowded beaches, packed clubs, social media parodies, people filming each other and Florida-style chaos. Rockstar's marketing has been selling *density* and *personality*, not acreage. That tells me the studio understands what made its best work great.

It's also why I'm more interested in Grassrivers and Mount Kalaga than in another skyline. The quiet parts of a Rockstar world are where its craft shows.

## The risk

Density is expensive. It eats CPU time, which is part of why technical analysts expect a 30fps-class console experience. If Rockstar has made that trade on purpose, choosing a fuller world over a higher frame rate, I think it's defensible, but it has to pay off. A world that runs at 30 and *feels* alive is worth it. A world that runs at 30 and feels empty would be a disaster.

## My test on November 19

I'm not going to look for the edge of the map. I'm going to park somewhere ordinary in Vice City and watch. If the street is still interesting after ten minutes of doing nothing, Rockstar has won.

Thirteen years is a long time to build a place. I don't need it to be the biggest. I need it to feel like somewhere people live.
