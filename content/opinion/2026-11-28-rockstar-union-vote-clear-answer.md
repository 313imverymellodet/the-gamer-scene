Let's start with the number, because it deserves respect.

Out of 1,544 Rockstar UK employees who voted, **1,163 said no** to union recognition, [according to dlcompare](/news/2026-11-28-rockstar-union-vote-fails-75-percent). That's **75.3%**, on a turnout of **93.2%**.

Those aren't a few loud voices or an apathetic workforce that didn't bother to show up. That's almost everyone voting, and three out of four of them giving the same answer.

If you support unions in games, as I generally do, the worst thing you can do now is pretend this didn't happen, or explain it away. It happened. Rockstar's UK developers were asked a direct question, and they answered it.

## Why people might have voted no

It's worth thinking about why, rather than assuming.

Some Rockstar staff may genuinely be happy with how they're treated. Rockstar has worked hard on its reputation since the crunch controversies around Red Dead Redemption 2. Some developers have publicly defended it as a good place to work.

Some may have had doubts about this specific union, or this specific agreement. Voting against one deal isn't the same as rejecting the idea of collective bargaining forever.

And the timing was extraordinary. The ballot ran during the exact two weeks that GTA 6 shipped, hit a 99 on Metacritic and made [$3.79 billion on day one](/news/2026-11-23-gta-6-sales-3-79-billion-biggest-launch-ever). It's hard to imagine a moment when people would feel more proud of their employer, or more cautious about rocking the boat.

## What Rockstar shouldn't take from it

Rockstar's statement said the result shows strong support for its direct relationship with staff. Fine. But a no vote isn't a blank check.

The bigger dispute is still open. More than 30 workers were fired in October 2025. Rockstar says they leaked confidential information. They say they were fired for organizing. [A tribunal still has to rule](/news/2026-11-18-rockstar-union-tribunal-ends-gta-6-launch). Nothing about this vote answers that question, and it would be wrong for anyone, on either side, to suggest otherwise.

The issues the union raised also haven't gone away: overtime, pay transparency, layoffs and the use of AI. Those concerns are real across the whole industry, especially in a month when [Firaxis](/news/2026-11-23-firaxis-layoffs-third-of-staff), [Ubisoft](/news/2026-11-23-ubisoft-166-layoffs-quebec-sales-fall) and [Reflector](/news/2026-11-28-bandai-namco-closing-reflector-unknown-9) all lost jobs.

If Rockstar wants to argue that direct relationships work better than collective bargaining, the burden is now on Rockstar to prove it. That means fair pay, transparency about crunch, and honesty about how AI will be used. Developers will remember if the promise of "direct relationships" turns out to mean nothing.

## What the union should take from it

The IWGB says the result is "a sign to double down." I'd suggest something slightly different: a sign to listen.

A union can't succeed by telling three-quarters of a workforce they voted the wrong way. It has to understand what they want, and why this offer didn't persuade them. That's slower and less satisfying than a big campaign. It's also the only way this ever changes.

## The bottom line

This was a clear answer from Rockstar's UK staff, and everyone should respect it.

It just isn't the final word on whether game developers, at Rockstar or anywhere else, need a stronger voice. That question will come back, probably the next time the industry has a bad year. And in games, the next bad year is never far away.
